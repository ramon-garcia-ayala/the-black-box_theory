Chapter 2 — g02 · Ignorance & Worship
Slide 1 · Mg01_g02_01
intro.txt
In a state of ignorance, technical objects appear to us as black boxes. We do not understand their internal processes, their logic, or their evolution. We simply see the input and the output — they seem almost magical, quietly producing exactly the result we need. Simondon does not simply describe this as a lack of knowledge. This ignorance, or avoidance, can actually be seen through two different ways. Let's look into those images:
Slide 2 · Mg01_g02_02
intro.txt
What do you see? By Barthes, the Citroën seems as a Goddess. Respected, worshipped, almost sacred. Other examples include the last iPhone release, or most recently the AP × Swatch collaboration.
Slide 3 · Mg01_g02_03
intro.txt
And now let's look at this. In the case of AI, data centers and computational infrastructure are often kept far from view, almost invisible to the people who use them every day. This is where Simondon identifies two opposite yet closely related ways of avoiding a genuine understanding of technical objects. We either ignore them completely, treating them as black boxes whose inner workings do not concern us. Or we elevate them to something almost mythical, attributing to them powers they do not actually possess. In both cases, the result is the same: we fail to understand the machine itself. We either dismiss it or worship it, but rarely do we engage with it. Which brings us to our next revelation.
Slide 4 · Mg01_g02_04
intro.txt
It is not humanity that has become enslaved to machines. The machines have been reduced to the status of servants — subordinated entirely to human needs and desires, as we continue being completely indifferent to what they are.
Chapter 3 — g03 · Opening the Box
Slide 1 · Mg01_g03_01
intro.txt
To understand this, let's bring it to an example. As we have del Campo with us today, let's bring it to AI. Let's take a look at the Robot Garden — an outdoor garden at the University of Michigan's Robotics building, inaugurated in 2021. It is revolutionary because the design of the ground and the textures of the garden were not designed by an architect sitting with a mouse. What they did was take satellite images of the terrain, train a neural network with thousands of images of architectural elements, and then let the network dream those elements onto the image of the site.
Slide 2 · Mg01_g03_02
intro.txt
When AI designs a building, something weird and interesting happens — and it is too weird and interesting to simply dismiss. The result is a landscape that looks natural but has a strange artificiality. The forms are not what a human would have chosen. They are what a machine saw when it tried to see architecture in an empty terrain. Which leads us to our next chapter:
Slide 3 · Mg01_g03_03
intro.txt
What if we open the black box?

Chapter 5 — g05 · Margin of Indetermination
Slide 1 · Mg02_g05_01
note.txt
Simondon noticed that when engineers try to make a machine "perfect" — fully automated, self-contained, optimized for one task — something strange happens. The machine becomes less technically sophisticated, not more. A robot that does exactly one thing, flawlessly, has no room left inside it. It is closed.
Slide 2 · Mg02_g05_02
note.txt
The machines Simondon considered truly advanced were the ones that had a kind of deliberate incompleteness built in — gaps where the world could enter, where a human could intervene, where the machine could couple with other machines or respond to changing conditions. He called this gap the margin of indetermination.
Slide 3 · Mg02_g05_03
note.txt
The margin of indetermination here is in the tool itself. The planer is not fully determining what happens. It leaves room for the material to speak, for the worker to respond. The tool is in dialogue with the wood and with the human simultaneously. That is where the skill lives — not in the tool, not in the hand, but in the space between them.
Slide 4 · Mg02_g05_04
note.txt
The planer doesn't decide what the wood will become. It holds open a question. And this is exactly where we arrive at one of the most uncomfortable questions in the room right now — Can a machine hold open a question of its own? Not just respond to the material. Not just transmit signals back to the hand. But actually reach into a space that wasn't there before and pull something out of it that nobody put in. In other words: Can AI be creative? Can something truly new emerge from a dataset of existing designs?

Chapter 2 — g02 · The Conductor Listens
Slide 2 · Mg04_g02_02
note.txt
THE CONDUCTOR DOES NOT DOMINATE. THE CONDUCTOR LISTENS. For Simondon, the good technician is not the master of a squad of slaves. He keeps each machine's margin of indetermination open — attending, adjusting, interpreting, never fully automating. To conduct is to listen: to hold the ensemble together without silencing any of its voices.
Chapter 3 — g03 · Situated Inference
Slide 1 · Mg04_g03_01
note.txt
DESIGN AS SITUATED INFERENCE In del Campo's "Intersections" paper, creativity is not a metaphysical spark but an INFERENTIAL process — ideation, evaluation, and contextual negotiation distributed across human, machine, and material. The architect becomes a MEDIATOR: selecting, interpreting, and translating the model's statistical relations into cultural and spatial propositions.
